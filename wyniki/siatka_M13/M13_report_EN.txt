Mesh selection

M13 was selected for the subsequent wave campaign as a practical compromise between computational cost, force-coefficient agreement and near-wall resolution. A project-specific limit of 5% was adopted for the absolute relative differences in mean hydrodynamic lift coefficient CL and total drag coefficient CD, each evaluated against the finest mesh in the comparison, M12. In the available final 0.2 s averaging windows, the signed differences for M13 were +1.15% in CL and -3.10% in CD. Both were within the selected comparison band. This band was a project screening criterion, not an estimate of discretization uncertainty.

M13 retained the near-wall mesh settings of M10: a first-layer thickness of approximately 1.53 micrometres, 48 nominal layers and 98.70% full-layer coverage. Area-weighted analysis of the exported foil-wall data gave y+ < 1 over 99.883% of the surface and a 95th percentile of approximately 0.800 for both meshes. Two small trailing-edge faces, together representing 0.117% of the wall area, had elevated y+, with a maximum of approximately 253. These local exceptions were retained in the assessment rather than excluded from the statistics.

M13 contained 309,441 cells, 73.19% fewer than M12 and 2.47% fewer than M10. Its time step of 0.4 ms required 2,500 steps per simulated second, compared with 10,000 for M12 at 0.1 ms; M10 also used 0.4 ms. Elapsed wall-time increments over the final 0.2 s of each run gave normalized costs of 34.7, 36.2 and 409.3 minutes per simulated second for M13, M10 and M12, respectively. The observed cost difference between M13 and M10 was approximately 4.2%. These short samples were not obtained under controlled system load and therefore do not establish a guaranteed speedup.

The selection supports M13 as a low-cost tested option within the M10-M13 comparison, with preserved near-wall resolution and force differences inside the adopted band. M10 remained a credible alternative because it had smaller force-coefficient differences relative to M12 at the same time step. Different time steps and final averaging times prevent the present comparison from isolating spatial discretization effects. It does not establish that M13 is the smallest possible mesh, demonstrate formal grid independence, or replace verification of the wave-response quantities.
